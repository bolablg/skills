# Prostory — project storytelling

Make the audience understand why the work mattered before asking them to care how it was built. For a short pitch, put the stakes and the speaker's contribution in the opening. Use the stack to explain a decision or outcome.

## Find the story

- Identify the target industry, role or audience, format, and what the story needs to demonstrate. For an unspecified professional presentation, draft a 60–90 second interview answer in the speaker's voice. If several projects are available, prefer the one with a relevant decision, clear ownership, and credible evidence over the one with the flashiest stack.
- Pull the facts from the user's notes and any supplied artifacts, such as PRs, design docs, demos, dashboards, or evaluations. Establish who was affected, what was at stake, what the speaker owned, what the team did, and what was delivered. A repository can show what exists; it rarely proves ownership, adoption, or business impact by itself.
- Find the decision that best shows judgment: a constraint or risk, a real alternative if known, why the chosen approach fit, and what it cost or left unresolved. Pick the one or two technical details that explain how it worked. If the notes only support implementation, explain what was implemented and why without inventing a debate.
- Ask only for missing facts that materially change the story, such as who owned the change or how the outcome was measured. If there is enough to write, draft now and list the most useful verification questions separately.

## Draft the story

Use this spine as a guide, not a set of mandatory headings:

1. **Stakes:** What was hard, broken, slow, or risky? Who felt it, and why did it matter then?
2. **Ownership:** What did you personally take responsibility for? Where did others contribute?
3. **Decision and action:** What did you choose or change, why did that approach fit the constraints, and how did it address the problem?
4. **Outcome and evidence:** What changed for users, operators, or the organization? How do you know, and what is still unknown?

If a lesson or next step shows judgment, add it after the result. Preserve the speaker's voice. For formats and before/after examples, read [Audience formats and examples](references/audience-formats.md).

## Cross-check every story

Run both checks before delivering any format, including a single résumé bullet. Apply them again when adapting a story for a different audience or adding a claim.

### Industry relevance

- Compare the draft with the target industry and what the intended audience needs to assess. Use a supplied job description, interview prompt, role rubric, or stakeholder brief when available. Identify the specific industry problem or role expectation the work demonstrates, then check whether the opening, decision, and result make that connection clear.
- If the experience comes from another industry, explain the transferable problem or capability while preserving the actual project context. If industry or role is unclear, use an explicit provisional framing and flag the missing target if it would change the story. Verify any industry-specific standard or expectation you rely on with an appropriate source. A source about industry expectations does not establish what happened on the project.

### Accuracy against shared sources

- Trace each material claim about the project's stakes, ownership, decisions, implementation, and results to the specific shared note or artifact that supports it. Check dates, units, baselines, measurement windows, scope, and attribution where they affect the claim. Distinguish user-reported facts from measurements you can inspect. When sources conflict, a source is inaccessible, or the evidence is incomplete, qualify or omit the claim and name the gap. Never present an inference as an observed fact.
- Separate observed results from estimates, targets, and expected benefits. Keep pilots and benchmarks scoped to their test conditions and distinguish timing from causation. When no impact metric exists, use the strongest supported proof, such as an acceptance check, a released capability, or documented feedback. State what a prototype demonstrated and what remains unmeasured.
- Use “I” for the speaker's work and “we” for shared work and team outcomes. Generalize confidential details for external audiences and keep private source references out of shareable copy.

Deliver the requested copy first. By default, follow it with two brief notes outside the copy. For **Industry relevance**, name the audience priority the story demonstrates and flag any material assumption. For **Source accuracy**, point to the evidence behind the main claim and identify any consequential gap. If the user requests clean copy only, still run both checks and keep any qualification needed for accuracy in the copy.
