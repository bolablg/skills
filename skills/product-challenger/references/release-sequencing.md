# Release sequencing: product wedges, not feature piles

Use this reference when a founder has a validated product direction but the vision is too broad to build at once. It is especially important for “super app,” marketplace, platform, ecosystem, or multi-module ideas.

## Core rule

Sequence **standalone customer outcomes**, not implementation layers. Each release must solve a real job for a reachable initial customer and earn an asset that lowers the cost or risk of the next release.

A valid release earns at least one of these:

- A repeat customer base
- A trusted acquisition or distribution channel
- A paid relationship or reliable payment behavior
- A high-frequency workflow and permission to remain in it
- Defensible operational capability or partner network
- Useful data with a lawful, trusted right to use it
- An integration or compliance capability required by the next job

If a later feature does not use an asset earned by the earlier release, it is usually a separate product idea, not the next release.

## Inputs

Require a current working brief and the latest Product Challenger decision. Identify:

- Long-term vision
- First country and initial customer
- First recurring job and current workaround
- Buyer/payer and initial distribution route
- Critical validated findings and unresolved assumptions
- Founder constraints: team, capital, operating capacity, distribution, and time

If these are missing, return a validation sequence rather than a software roadmap.

## Release 0: evidence before software

Use Release 0 when demand, willingness to pay, or delivery feasibility remains uncertain. It can be a concierge service, manual workflow, mockup, landing page, pre-commitment, paid pilot, or partner-assisted process.

Define:

- Assumption being tested
- Customer and channel
- Manual or low-code experience
- Pass threshold and time window
- Cost ceiling
- Kill, narrow, or pivot response

## Release 1: the entry wedge

Release 1 must have one primary customer, one primary job, and one measurable outcome. It should be useful even if no subsequent release is ever built.

Include:

- Customer and triggering moment
- One promise the product can reliably keep
- Minimum product surface and required human operations
- Acquisition/distribution path
- Initial pricing or commitment mechanism
- Activation, repeat-use, retention, and unit-economics metric
- Explicit exclusions
- Gate to the next release

## Releases 2–4: earned expansion

Each release should be conditional, not calendar-driven. Use a table such as:

| Release | New customer job | Asset earned from prior release | Minimal product | Gate to start | Gate to expand | What stays out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Examples of legitimate expansion logic:

- First release owns a merchant’s daily sales workflow; second release adds inventory only after repeated use exposes stockouts as a paid pain.
- First release helps clinics schedule a specific diagnostic service; second release adds payments after patients and clinics already trust the transaction path.
- First release creates a reliable delivery lane; second release adds financing only after repeat orders, verified merchants, and risk controls exist.

## Super-app guardrails

Do not build a super app because adjacent features sound synergistic. Add a new module only when all are true:

1. The first product has a narrow, repeatable customer outcome.
2. The next job is repeatedly requested or observed from the same reachable customer base.
3. A real asset from the first product gives a distribution, trust, data, or cost advantage.
4. The new module does not break the core experience or unit economics.
5. The product can support its operational, regulatory, and customer-service burden.

If these are not true, keep the idea in the opportunity map, not the release plan.

## Required output

Return:

1. Long-term vision and a one-sentence sequencing thesis
2. Release 0 validation plan, if needed
3. Release 1 product wedge
4. Three or fewer conditional expansions
5. A dependency/gate table
6. “Not now” list
7. Risks that would invalidate the sequence
8. The next 30-day action plan

Do not provide engineering architecture or delivery estimates unless the founder specifically asks. A release plan is a product and evidence plan first.
